    Core idea Fluid and Electrolyte Homeostasis

    The lecture covers edema and the disorders of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphate: reference values and thresholds, mechanisms and causes, clinical features, diagnostic evaluation and treatment.

    Lecture: Dr.Ghofran Ali Jasim — M.B.CH.B, F.I.C.M.S(path)

    Roadmap Units in lecture order

    Learning points What the student should leave with
    • Know the reference ranges and the diagnostic cut-offs for Na, K, Ca, Mg and phosphate.
    • Edema = clinically detectable ↑ interstitial fluid, driven by sodium retention; treated with Na restriction, correction of the cause and diuretics.
    • Sodium disorders reflect water relative to sodium: hypernatremia (water / hypotonic fluid loss) vs hyponatremia (excess ECF water from impaired water excretion).
    • Hyponatremia is classified by serum osmolality and by volume status; treatment depends on the type and on symptoms. Avoid overly rapid correction (osmotic demyelination).
    • Hypernatremia: correct the volume disturbance first, then the hypertonicity.
    • Potassium: hypokalemia = total-body deficit or shift into cells; hyperkalemia = intake > excretion or disturbed distribution.
    • Calcium disorders reflect the effects of parathyroid hormone and vitamin D on bone, gut and kidney.
    • Mg, K and Ca disorders can coexist; hyperphosphatemia produces hypocalcemia.
    • Treatment is chosen by severity, symptoms and clinical setting (oral vs IV, saline vs dextrose, diuretics, binders, dialysis).

    Edema

    Definition · mechanism · types · treatment

    Definition

    Edema — clinically detectable increase in interstitial fluid volume.

    Mechanism How edema develops
    • Develops when excess sodium is retained, either from impaired renal sodium excretion or reduced effective circulating volume.
    • Common settings include decreased myocardial contractility, nephrotic syndrome, and cirrhosis.
    Types Types of edema
    TypeKey features
    Peripheral edemaUsually affects legs, feet and ankles; may involve arms. Pedal edema is limited to feet/lower legs.
    LymphedemaMay result from cancer treatment such as surgery and radiation.
    Pulmonary edemaMay occur with heart failure or noncardiogenic causes including infection, ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome) and toxic exposures.
    Cerebral edemaMay occur due to trauma, stroke or cancer.
    OthersExample: macular edema.
    Treatment Edema — treatment
    • Sodium restriction and correction of the underlying disease.
    • Diuretics are the primary therapy: loop diuretics are most potent, followed by thiazides and potassium-sparing diuretics.
    Quick Review
    • Edema clinically detectable ↑ in interstitial fluid volume
    • Develops when excess sodium is retained: impaired renal Na excretion or ↓ effective circulating volume
    • Common settings: ↓ myocardial contractility, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis
    • Pedal edema limited to feet / lower legs
    • Peripheral — legs, feet, ankles; may involve arms · Lymphedema — may follow cancer treatment (surgery, radiation) · Pulmonary — heart failure, or noncardiogenic: infection, ARDS, toxic exposures · Cerebral — trauma, stroke, cancer · Others — e.g. macular edema
    • Na restriction + correct underlying disease
    • Diuretics primary: loop (most potent) > thiazides > K-sparing

    Hypernatremia

    Definition · causes · diagnostic evaluation · treatment

    Definition

    Serum sodium >145 mEq/L (>145 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Why it happens & what it causes
    • Serum sodium >145 mmol/L, results from water loss or hypotonic fluid loss; less commonly from hypertonic fluid administration or sodium ingestion.
    • Clinical Neurologic symptoms include weakness, lethargy, restlessness, irritability and confusion.
    • Warning Rapidly developing hypernatremia may cause twitching, seizures, coma and death.
    Causes Causes of hypernatremia (4 mechanisms)
    MechanismExamples
    1 · Loss of hypotonic fluidRenal: loop/thiazide diuretics, osmotic diuresis, postobstructive diuresis, diuretic phase of ATN (acute tubular necrosis).
    Nonrenal: vomiting, diarrhea, GI drainage/fistulae, sweating, burns.
    2 · Pure-water deficitReduced water access/intake, neurologic or psychologic disease, respiratory water loss, AVP deficiency (diabetes insipidus).
    3 · Hypertonic sodium gainHypertonic electrolyte solutions, sodium bicarbonate, sodium ingestion, sodium modeling in hemodialysis.
    4 · Water shift into cellsExtreme exercise or seizures due to increased intracellular osmoles.

    Pure-water deficit = water loss without matching sodium loss (a mechanism of hypernatremia).

    Diagnosis Hypernatremia — diagnostic evaluation
    TestPurpose / finding
    Serum electrolytesNa⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺
    GlucoseAssess glucose level
    Kidney functionUrea, creatinine
    Urine electrolytesNa⁺, K⁺
    Urine & plasma osmolalityDistinguishes nonrenal causes from renal water loss
    Urine output24-hour urine volume; polyuria prompts evaluation for diabetes insipidus
    Plasma AVPIf indicated; helps distinguish central from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
    Treatment Hypernatremia — treatment
    1. Recognize symptoms and identify the underlying cause.
    2. Correct volume disturbance,
    3. then correct hypertonicity.
    SettingTreatment
    Hypovolemic hypernatremiaBegin with 0.9% saline; after stabilization replace free-water deficit with 5% dextrose or 0.45% saline.
    Central diabetes insipidusDesmopressin.
    Nephrogenic diabetes insipidusThiazide diuretic plus dietary sodium restriction.
    Sodium overloadLoop diuretics plus 5% dextrose.
    Quick Review
    • Hypernatremia serum Na >145 mmol/L (>145 mEq/L)
    • Water loss or hypotonic fluid loss; less commonly hypertonic fluid administration or sodium ingestion
    • Neurologic: weakness, lethargy, restlessness, irritability, confusion
    • Rapid onset: twitching, seizures, coma, death
    • Loss of hypotonic fluid — Renal: loop/thiazide diuretics, osmotic diuresis, postobstructive diuresis, diuretic phase of ATN. Nonrenal: vomiting, diarrhea, GI drainage/fistulae, sweating, burns
    • Pure-water deficit — reduced water access/intake, neurologic or psychologic disease, respiratory water loss, AVP deficiency (diabetes insipidus)
    • Hypertonic sodium gain — hypertonic electrolyte solutions, sodium bicarbonate, sodium ingestion, sodium modeling in hemodialysis
    • Water shift into cells — extreme exercise or seizures (↑ intracellular osmoles)
    • Work-up: Serum electrolytes (Na, K, Ca) · glucose · kidney function (urea, creatinine) · Urine electrolytes (Na, K) · urine & plasma osmolality · 24-h urine volume · plasma AVP if indicated
    • Management sequence: 1. Recognize symptoms and identify the underlying cause → 2. Correct volume disturbance → 3. Correct hypertonicity
    • Hypovolemic: start with 0.9% saline → after stabilization replace free-water deficit with 5% dextrose or 0.45% saline
    • Central DI: desmopressin · Nephrogenic DI: thiazide + dietary Na restriction · Sodium overload: loop diuretics + 5% dextrose

    Hyponatremia

    Definition & grades · tonicity · classification · lab tests · treatment

    Definition

    Serum sodium <135 mmol/L

    Mild130–134 Moderate125–129 Profound<125 mmol/L
    Mechanism Why it happens
    • Excess extracellular water relative to sodium due to impaired water excretion.
    • May result from non-osmotic AVP release in hypovolemia, CHF, nephrosis, cirrhosis or SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion).
    • Severity depends on magnitude and rapidity of onset.
    Concept Tonicity — cell response (lecture figure)
    Hypotonic

    Solute concentration inside the cell is HIGHER.

    Water is transported into the cell.

    The cells inflate and eventually burst.

    Isotonic

    Solute concentration inside the cell is Equal to the solution outside the cell.

    Amount of water transported into the cell equal to the amount of water transported out from the cell.

    Hypertonic

    Solute concentration inside the cell is LOWER.

    Water is transported out from the cell.

    The cells shrink.

    Types Classification of hyponatremia
    TypeDescription
    IsotonicHyponatremia with isotonic serum osmolality.
    HypertonicHyponatremia with hypertonic serum osmolality.
    Hypotonic — hypovolemicLoss of ECF volume and sodium; sodium loss exceeds water loss; may occur with thiazides.
    Hypotonic — euvolemicNormal/slightly decreased ECF sodium with increased total body water; commonly SIADH.
    Hypotonic — hypervolemicIncreased ECF volume with impaired renal sodium/water excretion; e.g., cirrhosis, heart failure, nephrotic syndrome.
    Diagnosis Hyponatremia — essential laboratory tests
    TestClinical role
    Urine osmolalityDifferentiates impaired free-water excretion from primary polydipsia.
    Serum osmolalityDifferentiates true hyponatremia from pseudohyponatremia or hypertonic hyponatremia.
    Urinary sodium concentrationHelps distinguish hypovolemic hyponatremia from SIADH.
    Treatment Hyponatremia — treatment
    SettingTreatment
    Severe symptoms100 mL of 3% NaCl IV over 10 minutes, repeated up to ×3 as needed.
    Mild–moderate symptoms at low herniation risk3% NaCl at 0.5–2 mL/kg/h.
    SIADH3% saline plus a loop diuretic; other options in selected cases include sodium chloride/urea tablets, demeclocycline or vaptans.
    Hypovolemic hypotonic hyponatremia0.9% saline.
    Hypervolemic hypotonic hyponatremia3% saline, fluid restriction and loop diuretic.

    Caution Avoid overly rapid correction because of osmotic demyelination risk.

    Quick Review
    • Hyponatremia serum Na <135 mmol/L · Mild 130–134 · Moderate 125–129 · Profound <125 mmol/L
    • Excess extracellular water relative to sodium, from impaired water excretion
    • Non-osmotic AVP release in hypovolemia, CHF, nephrosis, cirrhosis or SIADH
    • Severity depends on magnitude and rapidity of onset
    • Hypotonic — solute higher inside the cell; water enters; cells inflate and eventually burst · Isotonic — solute equal inside and outside; water in = water out · Hypertonic — solute lower inside the cell; water leaves; cells shrink
    • Isotonic — isotonic serum osmolality · Hypertonic — hypertonic serum osmolality
    • Hypotonic – hypovolemic — loss of ECF volume and Na; Na loss > water loss; may occur with thiazides
    • Hypotonic – euvolemic — normal/slightly ↓ ECF Na with ↑ total body water; commonly SIADH
    • Hypotonic – hypervolemic — ↑ ECF volume with impaired renal Na/water excretion; cirrhosis, heart failure, nephrotic syndrome
    • Severe symptoms: 100 mL of 3% NaCl IV over 10 min, repeated up to ×3
    • Mild–moderate symptoms, low herniation risk: 3% NaCl 0.5–2 mL/kg/h
    • Throughout: avoid overly rapid correction (osmotic demyelination)
    • SIADH: 3% saline + loop diuretic; selected cases: NaCl/urea tablets, demeclocycline, vaptans
    • Hypovolemic hypotonic: 0.9% saline · Hypervolemic hypotonic: 3% saline, fluid restriction, loop diuretic

    Hypokalemia

    Definition & grades · causes · drug-induced · treatment

    Definition

    Serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L

    Moderate2.5–3.0 Severe<2.5
    Mechanism Why it happens & what it causes
    • Results from total-body potassium deficit or shift of potassium into cells.
    • Common causes include loop/thiazide diuretics, diarrhea, vomiting and hypomagnesemia.
    • Clinical Moderate disease may cause muscle weakness, cramping, malaise and myalgia; Severe severe disease may produce arrhythmias and ECG changes.
    Causes Drug-induced hypokalemia
    MechanismExamples
    Transcellular shiftTocolytic agents, theophylline, caffeine, insulin overdose
    Enhanced renal excretionDiuretics, high-dose penicillins, mineralocorticoids, aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin
    Enhanced fecal eliminationLaxatives, sorbitol
    Treatment Hypokalemia — treatment
    • Mild deficits in chronic loop/thiazide use may require 40–100 mEq potassium.
    • Oral potassium chloride is preferred whenever possible.
    • IV potassium is used for severe hypokalemia, symptoms, or inability to tolerate oral therapy.

    Caution IV potassium should be administered in saline because dextrose may stimulate insulin and worsen intracellular potassium shift.

    Quick Review
    • Hypokalemia serum K <3.5 mEq/L · Moderate 2.5–3.0 · Severe <2.5
    • Total-body K deficit or shift of K into cells
    • Causes: loop/thiazide diuretics, diarrhea, vomiting, hypomagnesemia
    • Moderate: muscle weakness, cramping, malaise, myalgia
    • Severe: arrhythmias and ECG changes
    • Transcellular shift — tocolytic agents, theophylline, caffeine, insulin overdose · Enhanced renal excretion — diuretics, high-dose penicillins, mineralocorticoids, aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin · Enhanced fecal elimination — laxatives, sorbitol
    • Mild deficit in chronic loop/thiazide use: 40–100 mEq K
    • Oral KCl preferred
    • IV K for severe, symptomatic, or oral intolerance
    • Give IV K in saline — dextrose may stimulate insulin and worsen intracellular K shift

    Hyperkalemia

    Definition · mechanisms · treatment options

    Definition

    Serum potassium >5 mEq/L (>5 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Why it happens & what it causes
    • Develops when potassium intake exceeds excretion or transcellular distribution is disturbed.
    • Major mechanisms:
      1. increased intake
      2. decreased excretion
      3. tubular unresponsiveness to aldosterone
      4. redistribution to extracellular space
    • Clinical Often asymptomatic; palpitations and ECG changes may occur.
    Treatment Hyperkalemia — treatment options
    InterventionRole
    DialysisMost rapid method to lower serum potassium.
    IV calciumRapidly reverses ECG manifestations/arrhythmias but does not lower potassium.
    Insulin + dextrose / bicarbonate / salbutamolShift potassium intracellularly.
    Potassium bindersUsed for suitable asymptomatic mild–moderate cases.
    Loop diureticsIncrease renal potassium excretion.
    Magnesium sulfateUsed for arrhythmias from digitalis toxicity in hyperkalemic patients.
    Quick Review
    • Hyperkalemia serum K >5 mEq/L (>5 mmol/L)
    • Intake exceeds excretion, or transcellular distribution is disturbed
    • Major mechanisms: ↑ intake · ↓ excretion · Tubular unresponsiveness to aldosterone · Redistribution to extracellular space
    • Often asymptomatic; palpitations and ECG changes may occur
    • Dialysis — most rapid way to lower serum K
    • IV calcium — reverses ECG changes/arrhythmias but does NOT lower K
    • Insulin + dextrose / bicarbonate / salbutamol — shift K into cells
    • Loop diuretics — ↑ renal K excretion
    • Potassium binders — suitable asymptomatic mild–moderate cases
    • Magnesium sulfate — arrhythmias from digitalis toxicity in hyperkalemic patients

    Hypocalcemia

    Definition · causes · tetany · treatment

    Definition

    Total serum calcium <8.5 mg/dL (<2.13 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Why it happens & what it causes
    • Results from altered effects of parathyroid hormone and vitamin D on bone, gut and kidney.
    • Primary causes include postoperative hypoparathyroidism and vitamin D deficiency.
    • HallmarkTetany is the hallmark of acute hypocalcemia; prolonged QT may occur.
    Treatment Hypocalcemia — treatment
    Clinical settingManagement
    Acute symptomaticIV soluble calcium salts; calcium gluconate is preferred peripherally because it is less irritating.
    Associated hypomagnesemiaMagnesium supplementation.
    Chronic hypocalcemiaOral calcium; add vitamin D if calcium does not normalize.
    Selected casesRecombinant human parathyroid hormone may be required.
    Quick Review
    • Hypocalcemia total Ca <8.5 mg/dL (<2.13 mmol/L)
    • Altered effects of PTH and vitamin D on bone, gut and kidney
    • Primary causes: postoperative hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency
    • Tetany = hallmark of acute hypocalcemia; prolonged QT may occur
    • Stepwise: Acute symptomatic → IV soluble calcium salts · Chronic → oral calcium · Add vitamin D if calcium does not normalize · Selected cases → recombinant human PTH
    • Acute symptomatic: IV calcium salts (gluconate peripherally) · Associated hypomagnesemia: Mg supplementation · Chronic: oral calcium ± vitamin D · Selected: recombinant human PTH

    Hypercalcemia

    Definition · causes & mechanisms · treatment by situation

    Definition

    Total serum calcium >10.5 mg/dL (>2.62 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Causes, mechanisms & features
    • Cancer and hyperparathyroidism are the most common causes.
    • Mechanisms include:
      1. increased bone resorption
      2. increased GI absorption
      3. increased renal tubular reabsorption
    • Clinical Malignancy-associated hypercalcemia may cause anorexia, nausea, vomiting, constipation, polyuria, polydipsia and nocturia.
    Treatment Hypercalcemia — treatment
    SituationTreatment
    Mild–moderate, asymptomaticTreat underlying condition and correct fluid/electrolyte abnormalities.
    Crisis / acute symptomaticImmediate treatment; normal saline rehydration followed by loop diuretics in appropriate patients.
    If saline contraindicatedCalcitonin.
    Malignancy-associatedBisphosphonates; denosumab may be useful after suboptimal bisphosphonate response.
    Parathyroid carcinomaCinacalcet has been used.
    Selected causesCorticosteroids may reduce GI calcium absorption in myeloma, leukemia, lymphoma, sarcoidosis and hypervitaminosis A/D.
    Quick Review
    • Hypercalcemia total Ca >10.5 mg/dL (>2.62 mmol/L)
    • Cancer and hyperparathyroidism = most common causes
    • Mechanisms: ↑ bone resorption · ↑ GI absorption · ↑ renal tubular reabsorption
    • Malignancy-associated: anorexia, nausea, vomiting, constipation, polyuria, polydipsia, nocturia
    • Stepwise: Mild–moderate asymptomatic → treat cause, correct fluid/electrolytes · Crisis → normal saline rehydration, then loop diuretics in appropriate patients · Saline contraindicated → calcitonin
    • Malignancy-associated: bisphosphonates; denosumab after suboptimal bisphosphonate response
    • Parathyroid carcinoma: cinacalcet has been used
    • Corticosteroids may ↓ GI Ca absorption in myeloma, leukemia, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, hypervitaminosis A/D

    Hypomagnesemia

    Definition · causes · effects · treatment by finding

    Definition

    Serum magnesium <1.4 mEq/L (<0.70 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Causes & effects
    • Caused by GI or renal disorders, several drugs, and is commonly associated with alcoholism.
    • Clinical Neuromuscular and cardiovascular systems are most affected; ventricular arrhythmias are particularly important.
    • May coexist with hypokalemia and hypocalcemia.
    Treatment Hypomagnesemia — treatment
    FindingManagement
    Severe / limited venous accessIM magnesium may be used, though painful.
    <1 mEq/L or symptomaticIV magnesium.
    >1 mEq/LOral magnesium; sustained-release products may improve tolerance.
    Renal insufficiencyReduce magnesium dose.
    Avoid urinary potassium lossPotassium-sparing diuretics such as amiloride, spironolactone or triamterene.
    Quick Review
    • Hypomagnesemia Mg <1.4 mEq/L (<0.70 mmol/L)
    • GI or renal disorders, several drugs; commonly associated with alcoholism
    • Neuromuscular and cardiovascular systems most affected; ventricular arrhythmias particularly important
    • May coexist with hypokalemia and hypocalcemia
    • Severe / limited venous access: IM Mg (painful)
    • <1 mEq/L or symptomatic: IV Mg
    • >1 mEq/L: oral Mg (sustained-release may improve tolerance)
    • Renal insufficiency: reduce Mg dose
    • Avoid urinary K loss: amiloride, spironolactone or triamterene

    Hypermagnesemia

    Definition · causes · effects · treatment

    Definition

    Serum magnesium >2 mEq/L (>1 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Causes & effects
    • Usually associated with reduced GFR/advanced CKD; other causes include magnesium-containing antacids, nutrition support, eclampsia treatment, lithium, hypothyroidism and Addison disease.
    • Warning Increasing magnesium levels can cause sedation, hyporeflexia, coma, paralysis and respiratory depression.
    • Cardiovascular effects may progress from hypotension and bradycardia.
    Treatment Hypermagnesemia — treatment
    InterventionPurpose
    IV calciumAntagonizes neuromuscular and cardiovascular effects of magnesium.
    0.45% NaCl + loop diureticPromotes magnesium elimination in patients with adequate renal function.
    Dialysis modificationUse magnesium-free dialysate in dialysis patients.
    Glucose + insulinMay promote magnesium entry into cells; glucose prevents hypoglycemia.
    Quick Review
    • Hypermagnesemia Mg >2 mEq/L (>1 mmol/L)
    • Usually reduced GFR / advanced CKD
    • Other causes: Mg-containing antacids, nutrition support, eclampsia treatment, lithium, hypothyroidism, Addison disease
    • Rising Mg: sedation, hyporeflexia, coma, paralysis, respiratory depression
    • Cardiovascular effects progress from hypotension and bradycardia
    • IV calcium — antagonizes neuromuscular and cardiovascular effects
    • 0.45% NaCl + loop diuretic — ↑ Mg elimination with adequate renal function
    • Dialysis patients: Mg-free dialysate
    • Glucose + insulin — may promote Mg entry into cells; glucose prevents hypoglycemia

    Hyperphosphatemia

    Definition · causes · major effects · treatment

    Definition

    Serum phosphorus >4.5 mg/dL (>1.45 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Causes & effects
    • Most commonly due to reduced phosphorus excretion from decreased GFR.
    • Intracellular phosphate release may occur in rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis and tumor lysis syndrome.
    • Effects Major effects relate to hypocalcemia and calcium-phosphate precipitation in tissues.
    Treatment Hyperphosphatemia — treatment
    ApproachExamples / role
    Reduce GI absorptionPhosphate binders such as aluminum salts and calcium carbonate.
    Non-calcium / non-aluminum bindersSevelamer, lanthanum carbonate, sucroferric oxyhydroxide, ferric citrate.
    Severe symptomatic with tetanyIV calcium salts.
    Increase renal excretionLoop diuretics or carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide.
    Quick Review
    • Hyperphosphatemia phosphorus >4.5 mg/dL (>1.45 mmol/L)
    • Most commonly ↓ phosphorus excretion from decreased GFR
    • Intracellular phosphate release: rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, tumor lysis syndrome
    • Major effects: hypocalcemia and calcium-phosphate precipitation in tissues
    • ↓ GI absorption: phosphate binders
    • Binders: Aluminum salts, calcium carbonate · Non-calcium / non-aluminum: sevelamer, lanthanum carbonate, sucroferric oxyhydroxide, ferric citrate
    • Severe symptomatic with tetany: IV calcium salts
    • ↑ renal excretion: loop diuretics or acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor)

    Hypophosphatemia

    Definition · mechanisms · severe manifestations · treatment

    Definition

    Serum phosphorus <2 mg/dL (<0.65 mmol/L)

    Mechanism Mechanisms & associations
    • Results from:
      1. decreased GI absorption
      2. reduced tubular reabsorption
      3. extracellular-to-intracellular redistribution
    • Associated with inadequate phosphate in parenteral nutrition, chronic alcoholism, chronic antacid use, DKA and prolonged hyperventilation.
    • Severe disease can affect neurologic, skeletal muscle, respiratory and cardiovascular systems.
    Clinical Severe hypophosphatemia — clinical manifestations
    SystemManifestations
    NeurologicIrritability, weakness, numbness, paresthesias, dysarthria, confusion, seizures and coma.
    Skeletal muscleMyalgia, bone pain, weakness and potentially fatal rhabdomyolysis.
    RespiratoryRespiratory muscle weakness and diaphragmatic dysfunction causing acute respiratory failure.
    OthersCardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, hemolysis and increased risk of infection.
    ChronicOsteopenia and osteomalacia.
    Treatment Hypophosphatemia — treatment
    • Mild–moderate/asymptomatic: oral phosphorus supplementation.
    • Severe or symptomatic: IV phosphorus replacement.
    • Monitor serum phosphorus and calcium frequently in renal dysfunction or during IV replacement.
    • Vitamin D enhances intestinal and renal phosphate absorption.
    • Burosumab is indicated for X-linked hypophosphatemia in adults and children aged 6 months or older.
    Quick Review
    • Hypophosphatemia phosphorus <2 mg/dL (<0.65 mmol/L)
    • Mechanisms: ↓ GI absorption · ↓ tubular reabsorption · Extracellular-to-intracellular redistribution
    • Associated with: inadequate phosphate in parenteral nutrition, chronic alcoholism, chronic antacid use, DKA, prolonged hyperventilation
    • Severe disease affects neurologic, skeletal muscle, respiratory and cardiovascular systems
    • Neurologic: irritability, weakness, numbness, paresthesias, dysarthria, confusion, seizures, coma · Skeletal muscle: myalgia, bone pain, weakness, potentially fatal rhabdomyolysis · Respiratory: respiratory muscle weakness, diaphragmatic dysfunction → acute respiratory failure · Others: cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, hemolysis, ↑ infection risk · Chronic: osteopenia and osteomalacia
    • Mild–moderate/asymptomatic: oral phosphorus · Severe/symptomatic: IV phosphorus
    • Monitor phosphorus and calcium frequently in renal dysfunction or during IV replacement
    • Vitamin D enhances intestinal and renal phosphate absorption
    • Burosumab: X-linked hypophosphatemia, adults and children ≥6 months

    • Dialysis — most rapid way to lower serum K
    • IV calcium — reverses ECG changes/arrhythmias but does NOT lower K
    • Insulin + dextrose / bicarbonate / salbutamol — shift K into cells
    • Loop diuretics — ↑ renal K excretion
    • Potassium binders — suitable asymptomatic mild–moderate cases

    • Non-calcium / non-aluminum: sevelamer, lanthanum carbonate, sucroferric oxyhydroxide, ferric citrate

    Exam Focus

    High-yield points from the lecture — a final checklist

    1. All reference ranges and cut-offs (Na 135–145; K 3.4–5.0; total Ca 2.12–2.60; PO₄ 0.80–1.44; Mg 0.7–1.00 mmol/L).
    2. Hyponatremia grades: mild 130–134, moderate 125–129, profound <125.
    3. Hypokalemia: moderate 2.5–3.0, severe <2.5.
    4. Five hyponatremia types, and which test separates them (serum osm, urine osm, urinary Na).
    5. Four mechanisms of hypernatremia with their examples.
    6. Hypernatremia: volume first, then tonicity; hypovolemic → 0.9% saline then 5% dextrose / 0.45% saline.
    7. Hyponatremia: 3% NaCl 100 mL over 10 min (×3); avoid rapid correction → osmotic demyelination.
    8. IV K in saline, not dextrose; oral KCl preferred.
    9. Hyperkalemia: dialysis fastest; IV calcium reverses ECG but does not lower K; insulin/bicarbonate/salbutamol shift K.
    10. Tetany + prolonged QT = acute hypocalcemia; calcium gluconate peripherally.
    11. Hypercalcemia: cancer and hyperparathyroidism; saline → loop diuretics; bisphosphonates, denosumab, cinacalcet, calcitonin, corticosteroids by setting.
    12. Hypomagnesemia: alcoholism, ventricular arrhythmias, coexists with ↓K and ↓Ca. Hypermagnesemia: CKD; IV calcium antagonizes.
    13. Hyperphosphatemia: ↓GFR → hypocalcemia and Ca-phosphate precipitation; binders.
    14. Severe hypophosphatemia: rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure; burosumab for X-linked form.
